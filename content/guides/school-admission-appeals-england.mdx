Receiving a school offer you did not want is stressful, but a refusal is not always the end of the story. In England, parents have a legal right to appeal against a refusal of a place at a state school. This guide explains how appeals work, what the panel can and cannot consider, and how to prepare.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- You have a **right to appeal** if your child is refused a place at a state school you applied for. You can also join the waiting list at the same time.
- The panel is **independent** of the school and local authority, and its decision is binding.
- Appeals succeed on **specific grounds**, not simply because you prefer the school. Preparation matters.

</Callout>

## Who can appeal?

Any parent whose application for a state school place has been refused can appeal. If you applied for several schools, you can appeal for each one you were refused. You do not have to accept the school you were allocated in the meantime, and appealing does not remove your child's place there.

## The deadline

The refusal letter will say how to appeal and the date by which you must lodge it. Admission authorities must give you a reasonable time, and for the main admissions round that is at least 20 school days from the date of the refusal letter. Do not leave it to the last day. If you miss the deadline you may lose the opportunity to be heard in the main round.

## What the panel looks at

An independent appeal panel considers two main questions, in two stages:

1. **Were the school's admission arrangements lawful, and were they applied correctly to your child?** For example, was your distance measured accurately, or was a sibling link missed?
2. If the answers are fine but your child would be the extra pupil, **does the harm to the school of admitting more children outweigh the harm to your child of not getting a place?** This is called balancing the prejudice. The panel weighs your reasons against the effect on the school.

## Infant class size appeals

In Reception, Year 1 and Year 2, the law limits a class taught by one teacher to **30 children**. Appeals for these year groups are much narrower. The panel can only uphold the appeal if the admission arrangements were not lawful, they were not properly applied, or the decision to refuse was not one a reasonable authority would make. They cannot simply decide that one more child would be fine. These appeals are harder to win.

## Reasons that can help

Strong appeals focus on the child and on facts that matter:

- an **error** in how your application was assessed;
- a **specific need** the school can meet and the alternative cannot, backed by evidence from professionals;
- **practical hardship,** such as an impossible journey to the allocated school, with evidence;
- facts that show the school could cope with one more child.

Reasons that rarely help on their own: that the school is nearer or better, that friends are going, or that you simply prefer it.

## Preparing your case

1. **Request the school's and authority's case papers** in advance and read them carefully.
2. **Write a clear, factual statement.** Keep to the relevant grounds, in order, with evidence attached.
3. **Gather evidence:** letters from professionals, journey details, or documents showing a mistake.
4. **Plan what you will say** and keep it concise.
5. **Attend the hearing** if you can, and consider bringing someone for support.

## What happens afterwards

The panel's decision is binding. If you win, your child is offered the place. If you lose, you can usually stay on the waiting list and, in limited circumstances, challenge the process through the Local Government and Social Care Ombudsman or the courts if you believe the procedure was unfair, though you cannot appeal the decision itself again.

## While you wait

Keep your child's allocated school place and prepare for the possibility that it will be the school they attend. Join the waiting lists, which often move over the summer as families change their plans.
