Around the start of Year 10, many families hear the phrase "triple science" and wonder whether their child should take it. The choice between combined and separate sciences affects the number of GCSEs your child takes and, sometimes, A-Level options. This guide explains the difference in plain terms.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- **Combined science** covers biology, chemistry and physics in one course and leads to **two GCSEs** with a double grade such as 6-6.
- **Separate sciences ("triple science")** are **three** GCSEs: biology, chemistry and physics, each graded 9 to 1.
- Many students go on to A-Level science from either route, but **sixth form requirements differ**, so check.

</Callout>

## What each route involves

**Combined science** covers all three sciences in one qualification, worth two GCSEs. Students receive a double grade, for example 5-4 or 7-7, rather than separate grades for each science. It is the standard route for many students.

**Separate sciences** let students study biology, chemistry and physics as three distinct GCSEs. They usually need extra teaching time, and schools often offer them as an option or to students on a particular pathway. Each science receives its own grade, and the content goes into more depth.

Some schools offer a version in between, for example allowing students to take two sciences separately. Policies differ, so read your school's options information.

## Which suits your child?

**Interest and aptitude.** A child who loves science and is confident in maths often enjoys the extra depth of separate sciences. A child who finds science hard may do better by focusing on securing strong grades in combined science.

**Workload.** Separate sciences cover more content and take more teaching time, which may use an option slot or extra lessons.

**Future plans.** If your child is considering medicine, engineering, a science degree or a science-based career, separate sciences or strong combined grades are both common routes, but entry requirements vary.

## Does it matter for A-Level?

Sixth forms set their own requirements for A-Level sciences, usually a minimum grade in the science and in maths. Some prefer or expect separate sciences for subjects like chemistry or physics, while others accept strong combined science grades. The safest approach is to **check the sixth form's published requirements** for the subjects your child might choose. Our guide to [A-Level subject choices](/guides/a-level-subject-choices-and-university) explains how to research university requirements as well.

## Questions to ask the school

1. Which routes do you offer, and who is eligible for separate sciences?
2. How much teaching time does each route get?
3. What grades do you expect for entry to A-Level sciences?
4. How is the course assessed, and are there required practicals?

## Preparing for the course

Science GCSEs ask a lot, both in content and in maths skills. A little preparation before Year 10, and steady revision through the course, helps. Practical work, equations and exam-style questions all matter. Our [GCSE science tutors](/tutors/gcse-science) can build confidence in all three sciences.

## A few reminders

- **Grades matter more than the route.** A secure combined science result is more useful than a weak triple science result.
- **The decision is usually made before Year 10,** so ask your school early.
- **Plan beyond GCSE.** If your child has an A-Level science in mind, check sixth form requirements now.
