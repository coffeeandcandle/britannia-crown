A timetable turns a vague worry into a plan. A good one is realistic, flexible and includes rest.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- List **what needs revising** and by when.
- Use **short sessions with breaks**, and include rest days.
- **Review weekly** and adjust.

</Callout>

## Step 1: Know the dates

Write down exam dates and any mock dates, then count back to see how many weeks you have. Mark commitments such as clubs, family events and holidays.

## Step 2: List subjects and topics

For each subject, list topics and rate how confident your child feels. Mark weak topics for extra time.

## Step 3: Divide the time

Split the available time between subjects, giving more to harder topics and those with exams coming soon. Aim for a mix of subjects each day, so revision feels varied.

## Step 4: Use short sessions

Sessions of 25 to 45 minutes, followed by a break, tend to work better than long stretches. Plan a clear task for each session, such as "do 10 questions on fractions", rather than "do maths".

## Step 5: Include rest

Build in breaks, meals, exercise, sleep and free time. A timetable with no gaps is rarely followed, and tired students learn less.

## Step 6: Review

Check progress each week. Move missed sessions, tick off what is done and adjust the plan. Pay attention to what is working.

## Tips

- Put the timetable somewhere visible.
- Start with the hardest subject when energy is high.
- Leave a flexible catch-up slot each week.

## When to start

Earlier is usually calmer. A light start months before the exams, building up gradually, avoids last-minute panic. See [how to revise for GCSEs and A-Levels](/guides/how-to-revise-gcse-a-level).
