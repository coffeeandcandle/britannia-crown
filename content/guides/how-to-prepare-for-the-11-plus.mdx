Preparing for the 11-plus does not have to mean months of drilling. The aim is for your child to arrive at the test familiar with the format, secure in the basics and calm. This guide covers what to find out first, how to plan and how much help is sensible.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- **Find out which test** your child will sit before you buy any practice material.
- Steady, **short practice over a longer period** usually works better than last-minute cramming.
- Preparation builds skills and familiarity, but **it cannot guarantee a place**.

</Callout>

## Start by finding out what the test is

Tests differ by area and by school. Some use papers from publishers such as GL Assessment or CEM, and others set their own. The subjects, format and timings vary. Check the school or local authority website for the test, the registration deadline and the test date. Our guide to [the 11-plus and grammar schools](/guides/11-plus-and-grammar-schools-explained) explains how the system works.

## What is usually tested

The mix varies, but commonly includes:

- **English**, such as comprehension, vocabulary and spelling.
- **Maths**, including fractions, ratio and problem solving.
- **Verbal reasoning**, which uses words, codes and sequences.
- **Non-verbal reasoning**, which uses shapes and patterns.

Verbal and non-verbal reasoning are new to most children, so they are worth introducing early and gently.

## When to start

There is no single right time. Many families who prepare begin in Year 5, and some start earlier with reading and number fluency. Whatever you choose, leave time to find gaps and fix them before practising under the clock.

## A simple plan

1. **Check the basics.** Secure times tables, fractions and reading comprehension first.
2. **Introduce each question type** one at a time, with the easy examples before the hard ones.
3. **Add timing** only once your child understands the method.
4. **Do full practice papers** close to the test, so the format is familiar.
5. **Review mistakes** rather than only scoring the paper.

## Keeping it positive

Short sessions, breaks and praise for effort matter. If your child is dreading practice, pause and reassess. A calm child performs better than an exhausted one.

## Do you need a tutor?

Not every child does. A tutor helps most when there are gaps in English or maths, when your child finds reasoning questions confusing or when you want an outside view of readiness. See [how to choose a private tutor](/guides/choosing-a-private-tutor) and [how often a child should have tutoring](/guides/how-often-should-a-child-have-tutoring).

## After the test

Passing the test shows a child has reached the school's standard. In oversubscribed schools, places are then offered according to the admissions criteria, and you must still apply through your local authority. See [how school admissions criteria work](/guides/how-school-admissions-criteria-work).
