Many parents feel nervous about helping with maths, especially when methods have changed since they were at school. The good news is that you do not need to be a mathematician. What children need most is regular practice, a calm attitude and someone who is interested in how they are thinking. This guide gives practical ways to help, from primary through to GCSE.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- **Confidence matters as much as skill.** Children who think they are "bad at maths" tend to give up early.
- Little and often beats long sessions. **Ten focused minutes a day** can be more useful than an hour once a week.
- Know when to step back and **bring in a tutor**, so that homework does not become a battle.

</Callout>

## Start with attitude

Children pick up our feelings about maths. If you say "I was never good at it", they may decide it runs in the family. Try instead: "That looks tricky. Let's work it out." Praise effort and persistence, not just correct answers. Mistakes are how people learn maths, and it helps when children see you make them.

## Primary years: build the foundations

Secure number skills are the base for everything later. At primary level, focus on:

- **Number bonds and times tables.** Quick recall frees up thinking for harder problems. Short, regular practice, a few minutes each day, works better than occasional drilling. Games, songs and quick-fire questions in the car all help.
- **Place value and the four operations.** Make sure your child understands why a method works, not just how to follow it.
- **Maths in real life.** Cooking, shopping, time, distances and money are all natural practice, and show why it matters.
- **Language.** Many children struggle with word problems because of reading, not maths. Talk about what a question is asking.

## Secondary years: keep the gaps from growing

Maths builds on itself, so a gap from earlier years can make later topics feel impossible. If your child is struggling:

1. **Find the real gap.** Ask the teacher, or look at recent tests. Often the problem lies a year or two back, such as fractions, percentages or algebra basics.
2. **Go back and rebuild.** It may feel like a step backwards, but it speeds progress.
3. **Practise regularly,** with short sets of questions rather than one long session before a test.
4. **Ask your child to explain.** Teaching you is a powerful way to learn.
5. **Use mistakes.** Keep a note of errors and revisit them a week later.

## GCSE maths

At GCSE, maths is assessed by three exam papers at either **foundation or higher tier**. Foundation tier covers grades 1 to 5 and higher tier covers 4 to 9, so the right tier is an important decision for the school and family. Exam technique matters too: reading the question, showing working to earn method marks and checking answers. Our guide to [how to revise for GCSE and A-Level](/guides/how-to-revise-gcse-a-level) covers routines that work, and our [GCSE grades guide](/guides/gcse-grades-explained) explains what the numbers mean.

## Practical tips for homework

- **Same time and place,** with distractions away.
- **Let them try first.** Resist taking over the pencil. Ask questions such as "What do you know already?" and "What is the question asking?"
- **Stop before frustration builds.** A break and a fresh start often works wonders.
- **Check understanding.** Ask your child to explain how they got an answer, not just what it is.
- **Talk to the teacher** if homework is persistently too hard or takes far too long.
