Many students find GCSE English harder to improve than maths, because there is no formula to learn. In fact, English skills can be taught very effectively once you know what examiners reward. This guide explains how the two English GCSEs work and the habits that raise grades.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- Most students take **two** GCSEs: **English Language** and **English Literature**. They test different skills.
- Both reward **clear, well-evidenced analysis** that answers the exact question.
- The biggest gains usually come from **planning answers, using quotations well and practising timed writing**.

</Callout>

## The two GCSEs

**English Language** tests reading and writing. Students read unfamiliar texts, answer questions about language, structure and viewpoint, and write for set purposes such as descriptive or persuasive pieces. The exams are usually in two papers.

**English Literature** tests knowledge of set texts: usually a Shakespeare play, a nineteenth-century novel, a modern text and poetry. Students write essays about themes, characters and the writer's methods. Most boards assess it through exams.

Each has its own grade, from 9 to 1, and many sixth forms and employers expect at least a grade 4 in English Language. See [GCSE grades explained](/guides/gcse-grades-explained) and, if a resit is needed, [GCSE resits](/guides/gcse-resits-english-and-maths).

## What examiners reward

**Answering the question.** The most common lost marks come from writing about the text in general rather than the specific focus of the question.

**Evidence.** Short, well-chosen quotations, tied to a point, show close reading.

**Analysis, not description.** Moving from "what happens" to "how the writer creates an effect and why" is the step from middle to top grades.

**Clear structure.** A short plan before writing keeps answers focused and organised.

**Accuracy.** In Language, spelling, punctuation and grammar carry marks, and they make writing easier to read.

## Practical ways to improve

**1. Read more, and more widely.** Fiction, news and non-fiction all build vocabulary and a sense of how writers make choices.

**2. Learn the set texts well.** In Literature, knowing the plot, key quotations and themes frees up the time in the exam for analysis. Short, regular revision beats cramming.

**3. Practise timed answers.** Writing under time pressure is a skill. Do some answers within the real time limits, then compare them with mark schemes and examiner reports.

**4. Plan before writing.** Even a two-minute plan improves an essay.

**5. Use feedback.** Ask the teacher what stops an answer reaching the next grade, and work on that one thing at a time.

**6. Build vocabulary and technique.** Discuss how a writer's word choices, sentence structures and imagery create effects, rather than just naming techniques.

## Writing tasks in English Language

For descriptive and narrative writing, aim for a clear structure, varied sentences and precise, vivid detail. For persuasive writing, think about audience, purpose, tone and a clear line of argument. In both, proofreading at the end can lift accuracy marks.

## Helping from home

- Talk about books, films and news, and ask "why do you think the writer did that?"
- Encourage a regular reading habit, even 15 minutes a day.
- Help your child create a simple revision timetable for the set texts.
- Praise improvement in specific skills, not only grades.
