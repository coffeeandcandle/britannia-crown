Mock exams are a rehearsal for the real thing. Used well, they show what to work on while there is still time.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- Mocks **do not count** towards final grades, but they shape predictions and plans.
- Prepare as if they are real, so you learn what works.
- Afterwards, **analyse the paper** and plan the next steps.

</Callout>

## When mocks happen

Many schools hold mocks in the autumn or winter of Year 11, and some also run a second set in the spring. Dates and format vary, so check with the school.

## Preparing for mocks

- Make a simple **revision timetable** that covers each subject. See [how to build a revision timetable](/guides/how-to-build-a-revision-timetable).
- Practise **past papers** under timed conditions.
- Look after sleep, food and breaks.

## Why they matter

Mock grades are often used to produce **predicted grades**, which sixth forms and colleges use when making offers. Strong results help, but a weak mock does not close doors. See [predicted grades explained](/guides/predicted-grades-explained).

## Reading the results

Do not just look at the grade. Ask:

- Which **questions or topics** lost the most marks?
- Were marks lost through **knowledge gaps, misreading questions or running out of time**?
- Which subjects need the most attention?

Ask the school for a marked paper and feedback.

## Turning results into a plan

List three to five priorities per subject, then slot them into the timetable. Revisit topics with the biggest mark gains first. A tutor can help with technique and tricky topics, particularly in maths, English and science.

## Looking after confidence

A disappointing mock can feel crushing. Remind your child that it is practice, and that there is time to improve. See [how to support a child through exam stress](/guides/how-to-support-a-child-through-exam-stress).
