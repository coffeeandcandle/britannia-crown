When a state school has more applicants than places, it cannot take everyone, so it uses a published list of rules to decide who gets a place. These are called **oversubscription criteria**, and understanding them is the single most useful thing a parent can do before applying. This guide explains how they work, what the common criteria mean and how to read a school's rules for yourself.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- Every state school publishes its **admission arrangements**, including the order in which it ranks applicants when it is oversubscribed.
- Common criteria include **looked-after children, siblings, distance from the school, faith and catchment areas**. Each school sets its own order.
- Whether you are in the **catchment area or live close by** does not guarantee a place. Read the actual rules.

</Callout>

## Who decides the rules?

The body that sets admission rules is the school's **admission authority**. For community and voluntary-controlled schools that is usually the local authority. For academies, free schools, foundation schools and voluntary-aided schools it is the school's governing body or academy trust. This is why neighbouring schools can have very different rules.

All admission authorities must follow the national School Admissions Code, which sets out what is allowed and what is not. For example, schools cannot interview applicants or ask for financial contributions as a condition of entry for state places.

## The usual order of priority

The detail varies, but oversubscription criteria usually run in an order like this. Some groups have priority by law, and the rest is the school's own choice:

1. **Looked-after and previously looked-after children.** These children have top priority by law in every state school.
2. **Children with an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP) that names the school.** They are admitted outside the normal process.
3. **Exceptional medical or social need,** where a school allows it and supporting evidence is provided.
4. **Siblings** of children already at the school.
5. **Distance** from home to the school, or living within a **catchment area**.
6. **Faith criteria,** at faith schools, often requiring evidence such as a baptism certificate or a faith leader's reference.
7. **Feeder schools,** where certain primary schools get priority at a particular secondary.

The exact order, and how each criterion is defined, is what matters. "Sibling" might mean a brother or sister who will still be at the school when your child starts, not simply anyone who ever attended.

## How distance is measured

Many families assume "close to the school" is all they need. In practice, the school or local authority measures it in a stated way, most often a **straight line** from your home to the school gate, or sometimes walking distance. A few hundred metres can be the difference between an offer and a refusal at a popular school. The admissions policy will say exactly how it is measured and which address counts.

Last year's **furthest distance offered** is often published, and it is a good guide, though it changes every year.

## What a catchment area really means

A catchment area is a defined zone around a school that may give priority. Living in the catchment does **not** guarantee a place if the school is oversubscribed with applicants who rank higher. Some schools have no catchment at all and use distance only.

## How to read a school's admission policy

1. Find the school's **admission arrangements** on its website or your local authority's website for the year your child will start.
2. Check **who the admission authority is**.
3. Read the **oversubscription criteria in order** and the definitions, which are usually in the notes.
4. Look for **how distance is measured** and which address counts.
5. Check the **application process and deadlines**. Faith schools often need an extra form (a supplementary information form) in addition to the council application.
6. Find the **published admission number**: the number of places on offer in the year group.

## Choosing preferences sensibly

You list schools in order of preference on one application to your home local authority. Schools do not see your ranking, and listing a single school gives you no advantage. A sensible list usually includes:

- a school you would love, even if it is oversubscribed;
- schools you are realistically likely to get;
- **at least one** you are confident of getting, so you do not end up with an allocated school you did not choose.

## If you do not get your first choice

You will be offered the highest-ranked school on your list that you qualify for, and you can usually join the **waiting list** for higher choices. You can also appeal.
