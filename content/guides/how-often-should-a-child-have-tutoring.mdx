How often a child should have tutoring is one of the first questions parents ask, and there is no single right answer. It depends on what you want to achieve, how far there is to go and how much your child can take in without becoming tired or resentful.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- **Once a week** suits most children who want steady progress or confidence.
- **Twice a week** can help with big gaps or a close deadline, but only if your child has the energy for it.
- **Short bursts** before exams or tests can work well for children who are already doing fine.

</Callout>

## Start with the goal

A goal such as "feel more confident in maths" needs a different pattern from "reach a grade 6 by June". Write down what you want to change and by when. A tutor can then suggest a rhythm that fits, and adjust it as you see how your child responds.

## Weekly lessons

One lesson a week gives a tutor time to teach something new, set a small task and then check it the following week. It is also easier to fit around school, clubs and family life. For most primary and secondary pupils, this is the sensible starting point.

## More than once a week

Two lessons a week can make sense when:

- there is a **fixed deadline**, such as GCSEs, A-Levels or an entrance test, and several months of work to fit in;
- your child has **gaps from earlier years** that are holding them back;
- your child is **new to the curriculum**, for example after moving from abroad.

Watch for fatigue. A tired child learns less, and an extra lesson can do more harm than good if it crowds out sleep, play or rest.

## Short, focused bursts

Some children do not need regular tutoring at all. A block of four to six lessons on exam technique, a tricky topic or a transition, such as starting GCSE courses, can be enough. If this sounds right, say so when you first speak to a tutor.

## Practice between lessons

A lesson is only part of the picture. Small amounts of practice between sessions, agreed with the tutor, usually matter more than adding more lesson time. Ask what your child should do between lessons, and how long it should take.

## Review regularly

Check in every half term. Ask what has changed, what is still hard and whether the pattern still fits. Plans often need to shift as exams approach or as gaps close.
