Parents often ask whether a tutor should be a qualified teacher. The answer is more nuanced than yes or no. This guide explains the common terms and what to look for.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- Tutors **do not need** QTS, but it can be a useful sign of training.
- **Subject knowledge and exam board experience** matter a great deal.
- Ask for **evidence**, then judge the fit by trial.

</Callout>

## Common terms

**QTS (qualified teacher status).** Awarded to teachers in England who have completed training and met the standards required.

**PGCE.** A postgraduate certificate in education, a common route into teaching that often leads to QTS.

**Degree.** A tutor may have a degree in the subject they teach, which shows subject knowledge but not teaching skill.

**Enhanced DBS.** A background check, not a qualification. See [safeguarding questions to ask a tutor](/guides/safeguarding-questions-to-ask-a-tutor).

## What each background brings

**Serving or former teachers** know the curriculum, exam boards and how classes work.

**Subject graduates** can bring deep knowledge and enthusiasm, and may be closer in age to older students.

**Specialists**, for example for SEN, languages or entrance exams, bring knowledge that general tutors may lack.

## What matters most

- **Subject depth** at the level your child needs. A GCSE tutor should know the material well beyond GCSE.
- **Exam board familiarity**, so the tutor knows how marks are awarded.
- **Communication**: can they explain clearly, and do they listen?
- **Track record**: references or examples of progress.

## How to check

Ask to see qualifications, a DBS certificate and references. A trial lesson is the best test of all. See [how to choose a private tutor](/guides/choosing-a-private-tutor).
