Choosing to educate your child at home is a legal choice in England, and thousands of families make it each year for many different reasons. If you are considering it, or have decided, it helps to know what the law requires and how to get going. This guide sets out the first steps, in plain language.

<Callout title="In a nutshell">

- Parents are responsible for ensuring their child receives a **suitable, full-time education**, which can be provided at school **or otherwise**.
- If your child is on a school roll, you **tell the school in writing** that you are withdrawing them to home educate.
- You do **not** have to follow the National Curriculum, but you do need to provide a suitable, efficient education.

</Callout>

## What the law says

In England, education is compulsory, but school attendance is not. Parents must make sure their child receives an **efficient, full-time education suitable to their age, ability and aptitude, and any special educational needs.** That can be done by sending them to school or by home education. Local authorities have duties to act if it appears a child is not receiving a suitable education.

There is no requirement to follow the National Curriculum, set a particular timetable, follow school terms or hire qualified teachers. For more on the legal background, read our guide to [home education in England](/guides/home-education-england).

## Step 1: Think it through

Home education is a significant commitment of time, energy and often money. Ask yourself:

- Why am I choosing it, and is it for the short or long term?
- How will it fit with **work** and family life?
- What does my child want, and how do they learn best?
- What **support** will we have, from other families, tutors or an adviser?

## Step 2: Withdraw from school

If your child is registered at a school, you **write to the head teacher** saying you are withdrawing them to educate at home. The school must then remove them from the register and tell the local authority. You do not need the school's permission in most cases.

There is an exception if your child attends a **special school** under a local authority arrangement, where consent may be needed first. If your child has an Education, Health and Care Plan, get specific advice before deciding.

If your child has never been in school, you do not need to register with the local authority, although some areas operate voluntary registration.

## Step 3: Decide on an approach

Families home educate in very different ways. Some follow a structured timetable and a British-curriculum framework, some are relaxed and led by interests, and many use a mix. Think about:

- **A curriculum framework.** Many families follow a British-curriculum route because it keeps the path to GCSEs and A-Levels clear.
- **Routine.** Children, and parents, generally benefit from a rhythm to the week.
- **Resources.** Books, online courses and local groups are all useful.
- **Specialist help.** Tutors can cover subjects you would rather not teach, particularly maths, science and languages at secondary level.

## Step 4: Keep records

You are not required to keep formal records, but a simple log of what your child has done, along with work samples, is useful. If the local authority makes an enquiry, it shows what you are providing. It also helps you see progress.

## Dealing with the local authority

Local authorities may make informal enquiries to check your child is receiving a suitable education. You can respond in writing, with a short summary and examples of work. You are not generally obliged to allow a home visit or interviews with your child, although cooperating politely often makes things smoother.

## Exams and qualifications

Home-educated young people can take GCSEs, International GCSEs and A-Levels as **private candidates** at an exam centre that accepts external entries. This takes planning, because centres set their own deadlines. See our guides on [entering exams as a private candidate](/guides/private-candidate-gcse-how-to-enter) and [IGCSEs and GCSEs](/guides/igcse-vs-gcse).

## Socialisation and community

Worries about social life are common. Home-educating families usually build rich networks: local groups, clubs, sports, volunteering, drama, music and online communities. Plan some regular activity outside the home from the start.

## What does it cost?

Costs are mostly yours. There is no funding for home education in England, so budget for resources, exams, tutoring and activities. Many families find it more affordable than they feared, and others invest heavily in specialist teaching.
